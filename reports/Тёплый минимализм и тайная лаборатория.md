# Few projects fuse warmth, minimalism and curiosity

No project in these notes combines the full Ковчег concept: a lightweight browser interface, a walkable cultural space, warm minimal art, and a cabinet layer in which each artifact carries its own story, notes and labels. The parts are common; the whole is rare. The closest per-object match is the Francke Foundations' Wunderkammer web app, where each cabinet object has a voice the player must save, and the closest walkable-space precedent is a photogrammetry virtual anatomy museum that drew positive public user tests. The most transferable mechanics are object-as-door (each artifact opens its own section, as in *Edith Finch* and the Getty cabinet) and juxtaposition as provenance (Geneva's Mirabilia), both cheap to build in plain HTML. Players reward solving and assembling, but they skip optional lore and resent essential facts hidden in optional pages. The evidence is thin: most findings rest on search snippets and reviews, no formal study has tested a card or notebook layer, and no live warm museum or archive site was verified.

## Per-object stories appear in museums and games, rarely together

Museum web work uses the cabinet as a navigation device, while games use the object as the trigger for a story. Both traditions give each artifact its own text, but they almost never share one interface. The **closest match** to "each artifact has its own story" is the Francke Foundations' Wunderkammer app in Halle, a web app for children aged 6–12 and families. A crocodile character, Tayé, asks players to save "the voices of the objects, which have been telling the story of the world here for over 300 years" ([Francke Foundations](https://francke-halle.de/en/education/krokoseum/voices)). The project began in 2021 and ran under the federally funded "dive in" programme ([Francke project page](https://bildung.francke-halle.de/en/development/facilities/krokoseum-childrens-creative-centre/discover-the-wunderkammer-with-suddenlife-gaming)). The notes rate it **rare**, but both sources are search snippets from a domain that was blocked during research, so the format and the voice mechanic are unverified in play.

Geneva's **Mirabilia** portal, launched in 2020 by eight municipal institutions, takes the opposite route. It pairs artworks, archive documents and scientific objects that **would not normally appear together**, so the juxtaposition carries the story. Visitors browse by material, object type, country or institution, and can build and share their own virtual galleries ([Geneva city dossier](https://www.geneve.ch/actualites/dossiers-information/culture-creatrice-valeurs/culture-innovation/mediation-culturelle-outils-numeriques)). This is the nearest existing model for provenance as composition. The Getty's display cabinet shows the metaphor at its most literal: a virtual model of a 17th-century cabinet opens drawer by drawer, and each drawer and panel carries text on history, materials and imagery. The same interactive runs on getty.edu and beside the real object in gallery N102, and an AR version lets visitors spin, tilt and open it ([Getty teacher guide](https://www.getty.edu/education/teachers/classroom_resources/tips_tools/downloads/cabinet_teacher.pdf); [Getty AR](https://www.getty.edu/art/exhibitions/north_pavilion/ar/index.html)). Here the cabinet is the whole page, which makes it a model for layout, though it is one object, not a walkable room.

In games, the per-object story is a stronger pattern. *What Remains of Edith Finch* turns each family memorial object into a vignette in its own surreal or allegorical style, about 13 in total ([Press Start](https://press-start.com.au/reviews/pc-reviews/2017/05/01/remains-edith-finch-review-exploration-death-depth/)). *Return of the Obra Dinn* makes the player the cataloguer: a ledger of crew and passengers filled by deduction, with a pocket watch that shows the moment of death, and chapters deliberately out of order ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_of_the_Obra_Dinn); [StatelyPlay](https://statelyplay.com/2018/11/09/return-of-the-obra-dinn-is-a-goddamned-miracle/)). *Hypnospace Outlaw* leaves many pages unlinked, so they can be reached only through search, an unlock, or game files ([Filmstories](https://filmstories.co.uk/gaming/hypnospace-outlaw-the-indie-game-that-simulates-the-90s-web/)). Two titles come closest to a secret lab in a browser-style format. *Researcher* is an HTML5 game in which the player collects specimens to a standard operating procedure document, and four specimens keep the job ([itch.io](https://pneumajams.itch.io/researcher)). In *Test Subject 1601*, notes from earlier subjects are the clues to deeper secrets in the lab ([itch.io](https://raideraik.itch.io/test-subject-1601)); the notes do not establish its platform.

The literal "secret lab" frame is the rarest element. The notes found no museum or library project that presents objects as a classified research programme. The only title with that name, *SCP: Secret Laboratory*, drew reviews that, in the snippets retrieved, praise gameplay and team play and never mention the lab fiction ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/700330)). My reading is that the rare element of Ковчег is the fiction layer on objects that look real, not the cabinet itself.

## Warm minimalism lives in game art, and museums lag

*Gris* (Nomada Studio) gives the clearest example of colour as a structural key. Its chapters are organised around different colours, and the team worked from a full storyboard, "like an animated feature" ([PLITCH interview](https://blogs.plitch.com/en/blog/interview-with-nomada-studio)). For a cabinet, this suggests one key colour per room or era, so colour does the navigation before any label is read.

*Dorfromantik* has the most transferable art-direction statement. Its early style guide set rules for proportion, lighting and perspective, with the target "simple but warm and playful" and most visuals built from very basic shapes ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/how-dorfromantik-expands-its-cozy-world-through-minimalist-design)). A rule sheet, rather than a look, is what an artist can hand to a team. The notes found no sourced hex values, type pairings or motion curves, so the rules for Ковчег remain to be written. *Townscaper* shows that flat shading and simple lighting keep rendering cheap, which matters for a canvas build, though that claim rests on search snippets ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/how-townscaper-works-a-story-four-games-in-the-making)). *A Short Hike* began from a small autumn forest diorama built with "as few pixels as possible," and its palette was sampled from autumn photographs of the Canadian Shield ([Nintendo Everything](https://nintendoeverything.com/interview-a-short-hike-dev-on-bringing-the-game-to-switch-inspiration-art-style-possibility-of-a-sequel-more/)). Sourcing the palette from real material, rather than from a theme, is the transferable point.

Museum websites are where warm minimalism is weakest in the evidence. The notes verified no live, textured, warm museum or archive site. The Chester Beatty website by New Graphic is a candidate ([100archive](https://www.100archive.com/projects/chester-beatty-website)), but its content was not read. A Velora Art Museum concept chose ivory and burgundy to complement the artwork rather than compete with it ([Contra](https://contra.com/p/NZLgsloL-velora-art-museum-website-design)), and it is a concept, not a live site. My inference, not a sourced finding, is that the archive's palette should come from its own objects.

Browser-native tone exists in two small titles. *Sandspiel*, a falling-sand toy built with Rust compiled to WebAssembly and WebGL according to a search summary, runs in desktop and mobile browsers ([itch.io](https://maxbittker.itch.io/sandspiel)), and one reviewer calls it "the best falling-sand toy on the web" ([dinogame.gg](https://dinogame.gg/blog/sandspiel-review/)). *A Dark Room* is "worth its own browser tab," per a JayIsGames reviewer ([JayIsGames](https://images.jayisgames.com/review/a-dark-room.php)). Neither is a collection, so they show what a light browser build can carry, not how a collection should be organised. The only walkable-space precedent in the notes is *Monument Valley*, whose Escher-based geometry turns impossible architecture into a curiosity device ([Core77](https://www.core77.com/posts/26744/get-lost-in-monument-valley-the-mc-escher-of-ipad-games-26744)). It is a common reference and a native game. The notes found no walkable web museum that gives each object its own story, and that is the central gap for Ковчег.

## Object-as-door and fixed-field cards are the cheapest start

The mechanics with the strongest support are listed in Table 2. Each maps to a section, a card or a hit target on a page, and each has a caution drawn from the reception evidence.

**Table 2** lists the transferable mechanics.

| Mechanic | Seen in | How to build it in canvas or HTML | Caution from the notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Object as door | Edith Finch vignettes; Getty drawers | Each artifact is a hit target that opens its own section | The story should be the reward, not the only route to the facts |
| Juxtaposition as provenance | Mirabilia | Two objects in one frame; the pairing carries the meaning | Pairing without context may confuse; no reception data found |
| Fixed-field card with a found field | Objectspace cards; Obra Dinn ledger; Francke voices | Fixed fields (what it is, origin, date, curator's note); a "found" field (note or voice line) appears only after discovery | No verified example of a provenance field revealed progressively |
| Unlock by search | Hypnospace Outlaw; Her Story | Hidden entries surface only on search terms; cap results per query | Her Story reviews criticise the repetition of the loop |
| Unlock by quota | Researcher (four specimens); Francke saved voices | Count collected items; a threshold unlocks the next note | Keep thresholds small; no reception data found |
| Hold to reveal | Obra Dinn pocket watch | Pointer hold on an object shows a past state or note | Untested; the source is a Wikipedia summary |

The least common pattern, and the most buildable, is a curator's label, a player-filled card and a discovered note on the same object. Francke and Obra Dinn together cover it. The notes' own inference is to start with object-as-door plus a card, then add unlock-by-search for hidden sections, since Hypnospace and Her Story show that a web interface can carry both. Per-viewer progress, such as watched or unlocked markers, can sit in browser storage. Anything shared between viewers needs a server.

Gesture can carry the emotional beat. *Florence* uses drag, match and tap as metaphors for relationship moments, and completes a scene when the player stops touching the screen ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1025694/Letting-Go-A-Florence)). One reviewer excerpt says *Gorogoa*'s touch version makes panels feel like moving physical objects, though the search summary did not confirm which reviewer said it ([School Library Journal](https://www.schoollibraryjournal.com/story/gorogoa-thought-provoking-elegant-puzzle-touch-go)). Drag surfaces need `touch-action: none` on the canvas so the browser does not take the gesture. That guidance was read in a Chinese translation because the English MDN page failed to load ([MDN zh-CN](https://developer.mozilla.org/zh-CN/docs/Web/CSS/touch-action)).

Haptics should be an enhancement, not a core signal. Can I Use and the W3C implementation report both show that `navigator.vibrate` is not supported on iOS Safari ([Can I Use](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_navigator_vibrate); [W3C](https://w3c.github.io/vibration/reports/implementation.html)). A GitHub report on MDN's compatibility data claims it works, which is unverified ([MDN BCD issue](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/issues/29166)). An Apple Developer Forums reply attributes the block to abuse concerns, but that is a forum reply, not an official statement ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/760504)). No asset budget is verified. The only figure, a 170 KB JavaScript example, comes from web.dev's Japanese-language performance-budget article and is an example, not a measurement ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/performance-budgets-101?hl=ja)). For texture, canvas paper grain and CSS `background-clip` text were the sourced techniques ([Codrops author hub](https://tympanus.net/codrops/hub/author/sarasoueidan); the article itself was not read).

## Players reward solving and resent hidden essentials

Players reward the sense of assembling an answer. PC Gamer calls *Return of the Obra Dinn* "one of the best puzzle games on PC" and says it shows "real confidence in the player" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/au/return-of-the-obra-dinn-review)). An unofficial Metacritic mirror carries a user line, "I loved the satisfaction of slowly solving the ship's mysteries" ([Metacritic mirror](https://metacriticz.pages.dev/game/return-of-the-obra-dinn/user-reviews/)). Slow reveals survive when the payoff is clear. *A Dark Room* has a slow opening, but reviewers call it "pleasantly eerie and addictive" ([JayIsGames](https://images.jayisgames.com/review/a-dark-room.php)), and an OpenCritic reader found the plot meaningful "despite being doled out in tiny pieces" ([OpenCritic](https://opencritic.com/game/11322/a-dark-room/reviews?page=1)). A Pocket Gamer user comment calls the browser version "amazing" and the mobile version "not as good" ([Pocket Gamer](https://www.pocketgamer.com/googleplay/com.yourcompany.adarkroom)). That is one comment, but it points to the browser as a fitting home for this kind of piece.

The sharpest complaints concern hidden essentials and skipped lore. Critics of *Tunic* say that leveling and consumable information sits inside collectible pages, which "risks alienating some players" ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/tunic-review-clever-like-a-fox); [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/tunics-in-game-manual-gives-mechanical-importance-to-collectibles/1100-6509945/)). In *Subnautica*, the Databank entries lagged the game during early access, and "veterans stop reading them" ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/848450/discussions/0/4819392984420143897)). This is the strongest warning for Ковчег. A lore layer the visitor can skip is acceptable; a lore layer that carries needed facts is not. Interface friction shows up as well. One *Obra Dinn* reviewer felt "very overwhelmed" at the start ([Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/gaming/return-of-the-obra-dinn-impressions-review)), a blogger called its "annoying UX choices" the main flaw ([jmduke](https://www.jmduke.com/posts/return-of-the-obra-dinn.md)), and *Papers, Please* draws mouse-control complaints and unclear document management ([Honest Gamers](https://www.honestgamers.com/12056/pc/papers-please/review.html)). *Her Story*'s reviews criticise the repetition of its search loop ([Quarter Disorder](https://quarterdisorder.com/2015/07/09/review-her-story/)).

The only formal user test in the notes concerns a virtual museum. An interactive photogrammetry model of the University of Glasgow Anatomy Museum drew positive public user tests, and participants said they would consider visiting the real museum ([eprints.gla.ac.uk](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/195475)). The source-to-claim mapping is unverified and the sample size is unknown. A separate teaching app, trialled by 12 pre-medical students, led its authors to conclude that more research is needed before such apps can replace physical specimens. No test in the notes covers a card, notebook or case-file layer. The sharpest risk for a secret-lab layer is that it is believed. An AAP fact-check traced a "ritual abuse" report that circulated as genuine to a video game ([AAP FactCheck](https://www.aap.com.au/factcheck/ritual-abuse-report-is-actually-from-video-game/)). A document convincing enough to pass as real can escape its fiction.

The evidence base is thin. Reviews are self-selected, the Metacritic source is an unofficial mirror, and most quotes come from search snippets, because fetches for pcgamer.com, digitaltrends.com, thegamer.com, geneve.ch, francke-halle.de and web.stanford.edu were blocked. The browser-tactile reception rests on five or six excerpts. No itch.io player comments and no Reddit or Hacker News threads were read. No study compares card, notebook or case-file layers, and no measurements of asset weight or mobile frame rate were found.

## Conclusion

The novel finding is the intersection gap, and it holds across three traditions that rarely meet. Museum interactives organise objects but seldom give each one a story. Indie games give each object a story but seldom live in a browser with a warm palette. Browser toys are small and light but carry no collection. Ковчег sits where these meet, which is why the combination is rare and why no precedent can be copied whole. The build order follows from the evidence: start with object-as-door and a fixed-field card, since that pattern has the most support; add unlock-by-search and unlock-by-quota for hidden entries; and keep every fact a visitor needs in the default view, so that the stories are the reward rather than the route.

The uncomfortable implication is that the secret-lab layer is the part most likely to mislead. A cabinet of real objects with invented notes reads as documentation unless the design marks it otherwise, and the AAP case shows that fiction can be taken as genuine. The next pass should close the gaps the notes name. It should unblock the blocked hosts, play *Researcher*, *Test Subject 1601* and the Eldritch Study render, read the Chester Beatty site, and examine the unread leads (wishious.cargo.site's "Map of curiosities," jasoneppink.com's "The Wonder Cabinet," and the Merz Akademie "Digital Wunderkammer" project). It should also run a five-person test on one prototype card, which would be the first formal evidence on the layer the concept depends on.
